# Lexical Normalisation Audit

This document preserves the findings of the diagnostic audits conducted during the development of DramaScope v1.1. It examines historical spelling variations and potential lemma artefacts generated by the application's lightweight lemmatisation pipeline when processing the expanded Early Modern drama corpus. 

These audits are purely diagnostic; no corpus data or analytical processing behaviours were modified as part of these audits, and all original spelling representations remain preserved intact in the corpus source CSV files and textual evidence.

## Normalisation principle

DramaScope deterministically normalises historically variant forms only where the mapping to an analytical form is context-independent and sufficiently secure. Ambiguous forms are retained rather than forcibly modernised. Frequency informs priority and analytical impact, but does not determine whether an otherwise certain mapping is valid.

### Ambiguity & Context Findings

#### `bee` (Insect vs. Verb)
- **Shakespeare**: Represents the literal **insect** content word (11 times, e.g., *"’Tis seldom when the bee doth leave her comb"* in *Henry IV, Pt 2*).
- **Other Playwrights**: Almost exclusively represents spelling variations of the **verb `be`** (e.g., *"why weele bee most sumptuously attir'd"* in Jonson's *The Case Is Altered*).
- **Conclusion**: Mapping `bee` globally to `be` would turn a legitimate content word (insect) into a function word, deleting all semantic insect references from "Content Words Only" mode. It is retained as ambiguous.

#### `wee` (Small vs. Pronoun)
- **Shakespeare**: Represents the **adjective meaning "small"** (e.g., *"He hath but a little wee face"* in *Merry Wives*).
- **Other Playwrights**: Exclusively represents the **pronoun `we`** (e.g., Jonson's *Every Man in His Humour*: *"the best vse wee can turne it too, is to make a iest on't"*).
- **Conclusion**: Retained as ambiguous to preserve the adjective meaning in Shakespeare.

#### `hees` (Contraction vs. Plural Noun)
- **General**: Predominantly represents the **contraction `he's`** (67 occurrences, e.g., Marlowe's *Dido*: *"ah hees gone"*).
- **Jonson**: Used once as a **plural noun** (*Neptune's Triumph*: *"And of the Epicoene gender, Hees, and Shees"*).
- **Conclusion**: Retained as ambiguous.

#### `moe` (Historical spelling of `more`)
- **Occurrences**: Only 2 instances across the full 116-play corpus.
- **Occurrence 1**: Ben Jonson (*Catiline His Conspiracy*): *"Alreadie on foot, and rumors of moe dangers—"*
- **Occurrence 2**: Christopher Marlowe (*The Massacre at Paris*): *"Yet liues my brother Duke Dumaine, and many moe:"*
- **Conclusion**: Although extremely rare, both occurrences are contextually unambiguous spellings of `more`. Under the normalisation principle, frequency affects analytical priority/impact, not mapping validity; thus, it is safe to normalise `moe` → `more`.

### Unsafe Behaviours Found in `lightLemmatize()`

1. **Trailing Apostrophes Left by Possessives/Contractions (Category C)**:
   - **The Problem**: Words ending in `'s` (e.g., `love's`, `devil's`, `she's`, `lady's`) match the generic final-s plural rule. The lemmatiser strips the `s` but leaves the apostrophe behind, resulting in trailing-apostrophe tokens like `love'`, `devil'`, `she'`, and `lady'`.
   - **Why it's unsafe**: These trailing-apostrophe forms (e.g. `love'`) are treated by the dashboard as separate, unique words distinct from their root forms (e.g. `love`), inflating the vocabulary counts and fragmenting collocates and semantic results.
   - **Recommendation**: Add logic to either handle `'s` explicitly or strip trailing apostrophes from tokens at the end of the `lightLemmatize()` step.

2. **Aggressive `ies -> y` Reduction on Short Verbs (Category C)**:
   - **The Problem**: The `ies` rule unconditionally targets all tokens ending in `ies`, converting them to `y` (e.g., `dies` -> `dy`, `lies` -> `ly`, `ties` -> `ty`).
   - **Why it's unsafe**: This creates illegible 2-letter strings (`dy`, `ly`, `ty`) for common short verbs instead of their true base forms (`die`, `lie`, `tie`).
   - **Recommendation**: Guard the `ies` rule so it only applies if the resulting token has a length greater than 2 (or protect these common verbs explicitly).

3. **Malformed Adjectives/Nouns Ending in `ed` or `ing` (Category C)**:
   - **The Problem**: Words ending in `eed` (e.g., `speed`, `indeed`, `bleed`, `breed`) and non-present-participle words ending in `ing` (e.g., `nothing`, `anything`, `something`, `everything`, `changeling`) fall prey to the generic `ed` and `ing` rules.
   - **Why it's unsafe**: They get truncated into non-words (`spe`, `inde`, `ble`, `bre`, `noth`, `anyth`, `someth`, `everyth`, `changel`), which pollute the lexical frequency lists.
   - **Recommendation**: Explicitly safeguard these high-frequency non-verbs/nouns in the lemmatiser.
